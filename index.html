1. What the exam is, and what it will probably ask

Facts come from your Week 1 slides and the marking criteria. The prediction is my reading of those documents, not something your teacher has confirmed.

Predicted focus

Every stimulus source is about David: Samuel's version, Chronicles' version, Saul vs David, the Davidic covenant. Expect a question that makes you compare how David is represented, explain why using the world behind the text, then judge his significance.

Most likely question shapes
  • "To what extent do the world behind the text and the purposes of the authors explain why David is represented differently in Samuel and Chronicles?"
  • A hypothesis to judge: "Chronicles censors David" (Source 1 says almost exactly this).
  • "Evaluate the significance of David for the Jewish people and for Christians."

Whatever the wording, a top response does the same three jobs: explain the features of the texts, analyse the differences and what shaped them, then judge significance with an argument. Section 11 has eight practice questions covering each shape.

Same king, different story: what's really going on

  • Not a contradiction. Chronicles' readers already had Samuel and Kings. The Chronicler chooses what to retell. Think selection and emphasis, not lying.
  • Samuel asks: "How did Israel get a king, and why did it all go wrong?" It answers with warning and honesty.
  • Chronicles asks: "Who are we now, and how do we stay faithful after the Exile?" It answers with identity (genealogies), worship (Temple, Levites) and hope (David's line).
  • Both agree that God chose David, that the covenant matters, and that David's reign was foundational.

5. The Davidic covenant and why David matters

Your Evaluating marks (8) depend on arguing significance for individuals, groups and society. This section gives you the material.

The covenant

  • 2 Samuel 7 / 1 Chronicles 17 (Nathan's prophecy): David's "house" will last. God will raise up his offspring, establish his throne forever, be a father to him. David's son builds the Temple.
  • Psalm 89 (Source 3): "I have made a covenant with my chosen one… I will establish his line forever, his throne as long as the heavens endure."
  • It fits the earlier covenants: Abraham (promise of land and blessing to all nations, Gen 12), Moses/Sinai (law, "people of God"). The Samuel poster links 2 Sam 7 to the promise to Abraham.
  • The covenant with David is unconditional in promise but Chronicles also stresses that kings must be faithful to prosper (Source 4: "a king needs to keep up his end of the bargain").

Significance, by level

Individuals

  • David shows a believer can fail badly (Bathsheba), repent and still be used by God.
  • Servant leadership: humility, selflessness, compassion, empowerment, integrity, trust in God (Week 4).
  • Saul is a warning, David an example of trust.

Groups

  • Exiles/post-exilic Jews: Chronicles offers identity and hope: Davidic line, Temple, faithfulness.
  • Priests and Levites: David organises their worship duties.
  • Christians: Jesus is the Davidic king, "Son of David" (Luke 1:32-33).

Society

  • Model of how a nation is led under God: justice, accountability to prophets, covenant law.
  • Jerusalem becomes the political and religious capital for centuries.
  • Messianic hope shapes Jewish and Christian culture: kingship, music (Psalms), worship.

Priestly and kingly works (Week 4)

  • Kingly: leading armies, ruling justly, uniting tribes, capturing Jerusalem, administration.
  • Priestly: bringing the Ark, offering sacrifices, planning the Temple, organising worship. Chronicles highlights these much more than Samuel.
  • This distinction helps explain the two portraits: Samuel stresses the king, Chronicles the king as worship leader.

Messiah, foreshadowing and the "world in front of the text"

  • Messiah means "anointed king". After the monarchy ended, Jews hoped for a new Davidic ruler.
  • Christians read David as foreshadowing Jesus: "Jesus is thus the long-awaited Davidic king" (Source 3). This is the allegorical sense (section 8).
  • Samuel and Chronicles both prepare this hope. Chronicles' "new David" and the open ending ("Let him go up…") invite readers to expect more.
Judgement tip

"To what extent" answers are stronger when they argue a line like: Chronicles' version is significant mainly for the community and its hope, while Samuel's is significant for individuals because it faces failure honestly. Together they give the fullest picture of David.

Saul vs David in one paragraph (Source 2)

Saul has been abandoned by Yahweh and the narrator shows him dismayed by "unkingly fear", trusting in arms. David, in 1 Samuel 17, trusts God and delivers Israel from the Philistines. The writer places them side by side so that David's faith shines against Saul's despair. This shows that leadership in the Old Testament is judged by relationship with God, not by power.

Understanding character in biblical narrative (Week 6 video)

  • Biblical narrators say little about inner thoughts. We learn character from actions, dialogue, what others say, and what God says.
  • Characters are often complex: heroes have flaws, and failures can lead to growth.
  • Characters work as models or warnings (the Samuel poster: Saul a warning, David an example).

7. The Historical Books: Deuteronomist vs Chronicler

This is the bridge from Explaining to Analysing: it tells you what kind of text you are reading. Source: Introduction to the Historical Books (Catholic Youth Bible).

Distinguishing features of Old Testament historical texts

  • Sacred history, not modern history. Authors say "Let us tell you what the events mean in the light of our faith in God." They interpret events rather than only report facts.
  • Purpose over completeness. Authors select, arrange and omit to serve a message.
  • God acts in history. God cares deeply about what is happening in human affairs.
  • Reward and punishment. People are happier following God's law than turning away.
  • Multiple authors and sources, with stories sometimes repeated (Myth 3, section 8).

Deuteronomist (the "D" school)

Wrote or edited Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges, 1 & 2 Samuel, 1 & 2 Kings.

  • God rewards the good and punishes the wicked in this world.
  • Repeated pattern: (1) God's love, (2) people sin, (3) punishment, (4) people repent and cry out, (5) God forgives, (6) people sin again.
  • Measure of faithfulness: the Sinai covenant.

The Chronicler

Wrote 1 & 2 Chronicles and probably Ezra and Nehemiah.

  • Still reward and punishment, but measure of faithfulness changes: proper worship, especially at the Jerusalem Temple.
  • Wrote after northern and southern kingdoms were destroyed, to nurture a small community rebuilding itself.
  • Focus on Judah, the Davidic line, priests, Levites.
Christian perspective

The introduction reminds you that the authors' understanding of God "is not the same as Jesus' understanding". Christians read the historical books in the larger context of Jesus' teaching on God's unconditional love and unlimited mercy.

Basics

8. Interpreting the Bible

Learn these terms. A few correct, precise terms are an easy way to show "distinguishing features" and analysis.

Three Worlds of the Text

World behind the text

History and culture: who wrote it, when, why, what was happening. Method: historical-cultural criticism.

Samuel: Deuteronomist, monarchy and exile. Chronicles: post-exile, Temple.

World of the text

The text itself: genre, structure, characters, literary devices. Method: narrative criticism.

Samuel: narrative history with character contrast. Chronicles: genealogies, lists, theological retelling.

World in front of the text

How readers and communities interpret and use it over time.

David as Messiah's ancestor; Psalms in worship; Luke 1:32-33.

Worked example: the Shema (Deut 6:4-9)
  • Behind: Deuteronomy claims Moses as author but emerged over about three centuries, from the 8th century BC to the Exile. The "D" authors were probably priests or prophets. "Deuteronomy" comes from a Greek translation meaning "second law". Moses addresses the new generation at the edge of the Promised Land.
  • Of: The Shema is Israel's creed: hear, Yahweh alone is God, respond with love. It is a declaration of faith, not a prayer. Three parts: Deut 6:4-9, Deut 11:13-21, Num 15:37-41.
  • In front: Said morning and night, written on the mezuzah and in tefillin. Jesus calls its opening the first of the two great commandments (Mark 12:29-31).

Contexts to consider

  • Historical and cultural context (author's situation).
  • Literary form or device (history, poetry, prophecy, metaphor, parable).
  • Unity of the whole Bible ("use the Bible to interpret the Bible").
  • Sacred Tradition of the Church (bishops hold final responsibility, with scholars helping).
  • Scholarly "criticisms" (textual, source, redaction, literary, historical, canonical) are positive methods of analysis. They deepen the literal sense but are not enough alone. Redaction criticism (how editors shaped the final text) is the one most relevant to Samuel and Chronicles.
  • Exegesis = the process of interpreting a passage.

Samuel and Chronicles are mostly historical truth told as religious history. Differences in detail do not make the Bible false: both versions communicate religious truth for different audiences.

9. Background: salvation history, God, characters

Lower priority for the essay, but your timeline gives the setting for the "world behind the text" and helps with quick questions.

Salvation history timeline (Sprint Through Salvation History)

c. 1850 BC · Abraham. The Promise: descendants a blessing to the world, land of Canaan. Patriarchs: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob. Matriarchs: Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel.
c. 1700–1290 BC · Egypt. Famine brings Israel to Egypt. Later enslaved.
c. 1290 BC · Moses and the Exodus. God reveals the name Yahweh ("I am the One who is always present"). Escape through the sea. Covenant at Sinai: Ten Commandments. Forty years in the desert.
c. 1250–1000 BC · Joshua and the Judges. Conquest and settlement. Military leaders called judges. Shift from nomadic to agricultural life.
c. 1000 BC · David and Solomon. Israel a nation: David anointed king, Jerusalem the capital. Promise that his line will endure. Solomon builds the Temple.
922 BC · Division. Israel (north) and Judah (south). Heavy taxes, idolatry. Prophets call kings back to the Covenant.
721 BC · Assyria destroys the northern kingdom.
587 BC · Babylon destroys Judah and Jerusalem. Babylonian Exile. Second Isaiah: one and only God. Monotheism.
539 BC · Persians (Cyrus) free the exiles. Return, rebuild the Temple. Ezra and Nehemiah re-establish the Law. Jews now called "Jews" (from Judah).
330 BC · Alexander the Great. Greek rule; Maccabean revolt.
63 BC · Rome captures Jerusalem. Hope for a messiah, perhaps from David's line.
Jesus of the house of David. Christians see him as the Messiah.

The biblical period is about 1850 BC to AD 100, nearly 2000 years. Salvation History = the pattern of events showing God's presence and saving actions.

How God is portrayed in the Old Testament (Week 2)

  • God is described through metaphor (a figure of speech applied to something it is not literally true of): shepherd, rock, king, father, warrior, and also mother-like images (e.g. comforting as a mother in Isaiah 66:13).
  • Many images point to the idea that God is beyond human gender, neither male nor female. Using male images often reflects the culture of the writers. Using varied images shows that no single image captures God.
  • For the Week 2 question ("to what extent do the many manifestations of God… challenge or reinforce…"), a balanced answer: the variety of images reinforces that God is neither male nor female, while the dominant male imagery (king, father) reflects the world behind the text.

Jewish holy books, Bible structure and Psalms

  • The Jewish Bible is the Tanakh: Torah (law, first five books), Nevi'im (prophets, which include Samuel and Kings), Ketuvim (writings, which include Psalms and, in the traditional Jewish order, Chronicles last). The Chronicles poster shows Chronicles at the end of the traditional order, summing up Scripture "from Adam" to the return from Exile.
  • The Christian Old Testament places Chronicles after Kings among the historical books.
  • Psalms are poems and songs used in worship. Many are attributed to David. Psalm 89 (Source 3) celebrates the Davidic covenant. Other royal psalms in the Samuel poster: 2, 72, 132, 145.
  • Compiled during and after the Exile, Jewish leaders began collecting their ancestral writings, forming the core of the Old Testament.

Question: To what extent are the differing representations of David in Samuel and Chronicles shaped by the world behind each text?

The Old Testament tells the story of King David twice, and the two accounts feel like different men. This difference is largely, though not only, the result of the world behind each text: the situation, audience and theological purpose of the authors. While genre and the Chronicler's sources also play a part, the historical contexts of the Deuteronomistic editors and the post-exilic Chronicler best explain why Samuel presents a flawed but trusting king and Chronicles an idealised temple-builder.

The historical books are not modern history. They are sacred history, which tells "what the events mean in the light of our faith in God", so each author selects and arranges material for a purpose. Source 1 describes Samuel as the "unexpurgated" version, full of "sexual and physical violence, passion, scandal". In 2 Samuel, David commits adultery with Bathsheba, arranges the death of Uriah, is confronted by Nathan ("You are that man"), and watches his family fracture through Amnon, Tamar and Absalom's rebellion (Source 5). Chronicles, by contrast, depicts a "milder, tamer and more pious figure". Source 4 shows David praising God, seeking God's advice before battle, bringing the Ark to Jerusalem and drawing up the plans for the Temple (1 Chr 28:11). Source 6 confirms the key differences: persecution by Saul and the adultery and murder are "left out", while preparations for the Temple are "new material".

These differences are best explained by the world behind each text. Samuel belongs to the Deuteronomistic history, written and edited by authors looking back at the rise and fall of the monarchy, after the northern kingdom fell to Assyria in 721 BC and Judah to Babylon in 587 BC. Their conviction was that God rewards faithfulness and punishes sin, so they tell the truth about failure. Source 2 shows this theology at work: Saul, "abandoned by Yahweh", trusts in arms and despairs, while David trusts God and delivers Israel from the Philistines. Samuel's David is therefore both an example of trust and a lesson in the consequences of sin, a pattern the Deuteronomists repeat throughout the history. Chronicles, however, was written after the Exile, when Judah was a small Persian province without a king and with a rebuilt but modest Temple. The Chronicler's measure of faithfulness shifted from the Sinai covenant to proper worship at the Jerusalem Temple. Writing to nurture a rebuilding community, he omits the scandals that his readers already knew from Samuel and highlights what could give them hope: the Temple, the Levites and the Davidic line. Source 6 states the aim directly: to portray David as an image of the future messianic king.

Nevertheless, the world behind the text is not the whole explanation. Chronicles does not erase every flaw: it keeps David's census, though it shifts the blame to Satan and ends the episode with the purchase of the Temple's site. It also records that David cannot build the Temple because he has "shed much blood" (1 Chr 22:8). Meanwhile Samuel is not merely scandalous; Source 2 calls it "more sophisticated and theological". So Source 1's word "censored" overstates the case. The Chronicler is not concealing David's history from a naive audience but retelling it for a different purpose, and the Church reads both books together. This is the world in front of the text, where the two portraits join in the hope for a Messiah. Source 3 shows how Psalm 89 and Luke 1:32-33 draw on the covenant to present Jesus as the Davidic king, "despite his failings".

In conclusion, the representations of David are shaped to a large extent by the world behind the text. The Deuteronomists, writing about a failed monarchy, offer a truthful and cautionary David, while the Chronicler, writing for the returned exiles, offers a model of worship and a promise of restoration. Genre, theology and later interpretation add depth, but the audience and moment of writing explain the shift most convincingly. Read together, the two accounts do not contradict each other; they give believers a David who is both human and hopeful.
